Why does your phone need four satellites to find you?

Everything you will see is computed live in your browser.

1 / 8

A world the size of Earth

12,742 km across. The black block is you, holding a phone. Scroll to see how it finds you.

2 / 8

24 satellites, 20,200 km up

GPS satellites circle in six tilted orbits, twice a day, so several are always above you.

3 / 8

Each one broadcasts the time

Every satellite carries an atomic clock and sends out signals stamped with the exact time they left.

4 / 8

Time becomes distance

This signal took 0.07 s to reach you. Travel time × the speed of light = 21,000 km. So you are somewhere on a sphere that size around the satellite.

5 / 8

Three spheres meet at one point

Do the same with two more satellites. The three spheres meet at just one spot: you. But only if your phone’s clock is perfect.

6 / 8

Your phone’s clock isn’t

Satellites have atomic clocks; your phone has a cheap quartz crystal. Off by 1.00 ms, every distance is 300 km too long, and the spheres meet far from you. Every point on the orange line fits these signals, one for each possible clock error.

7 / 8

So time is the fourth unknown

Your phone solves for x, y, z and the time, so it needs a fourth satellite. It shifts its clock guess until all four spheres meet. Clock error found: 0 ms. Off by: —.

8 / 8

That’s why GPS needs four

Real phones use eight or more satellites, from GPS and other systems, to average out smaller errors: air slowing the signal, echoes off buildings. Even relativity counts: satellite clocks gain 38 microseconds a day, and GPS corrects for it.
