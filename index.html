Everyday magic

You’re driving somewhere new.

Your phone draws a blue dot on the right street, in the right lane, every second. That dot is GPS.

The blue dot

Your phone only listens.

It never sends anything to GPS. It picks up faint radio signals and works out where it must be to hear them as it does.

Pull back

The signals come from space.

From satellites 20,200 km up, more than three Earth radii away, each moving at almost 4 km a second.

The constellation

24 satellites, 6 orbits.

They circle the Earth twice a day in six tilted rings, spaced so that at least four are above you wherever you stand.

Step 1 · Time

Each satellite broadcasts the time.

Atomic clocks on board keep time to billionths of a second. Every signal carries the moment it left and where the satellite was.

Step 2 · Distance

Time becomes distance.

This signal took 0.07 s to arrive. At the speed of light that is 21,000 km, so you are somewhere on a sphere of that radius around the satellite.

Step 3 · Position

Three spheres meet at one point.

Two more satellites, two more spheres. Near the Earth, all three meet at a single point: you. At least, if your phone’s clock is perfect.

The catch

Your phone’s clock isn’t.

It runs on a cheap quartz crystal. Just 1.00 ms off, every distance is 300 km too long, and the spheres meet far away. Every point on the orange line fits the three signals equally well.

The fix

Time is the fourth unknown.

x, y, z and the clock error: four unknowns need four measurements, so a fourth satellite. The phone shifts its clock until all four spheres agree. Clock error found: 0 ms. Off by —.

In the real world

More satellites, smaller errors.

Phones also listen to Galileo, GLONASS and BeiDou. Extra satellites average out what’s left: the ionosphere delays signals by metres, buildings bounce them, and relativity makes satellite clocks gain 38 µs a day, which GPS corrects for.

Back on the road

All of this, every second.

Four or more signals, one clock error solved, one blue dot on your lane.
