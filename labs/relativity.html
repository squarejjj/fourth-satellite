Would you really drift 11 km a day?

38.5 µs of clock error is 11.5 km of light travel, so you often read that GPS would drift 11 km a day without relativity. But remember the fourth unknown: a clock error that every satellite shares looks exactly like an error in your clock, and the receiver removes it. What hurts is the part that differs between satellites.

Stretched orbits add a wobble

GPS orbits are very nearly circles, but not quite. On a stretched (elliptical) orbit the satellite speeds up when it is close to the Earth and slows down far away, and both relativity effects change round the orbit, so the clock error wobbles. Receivers correct it with one formula from the GPS specification.

GPS satellites are kept below about 0.02. The drawing exaggerates the shape fivefold so you can see it.

How it works
speed in a circular orbit v = √(μ / r) motion (special relativity) Δ = −v² / (2c²) × 86,400 s gravity (general relativity) Δ = (μ / c²) × (1/R − 1/r) × 86,400 s elliptical-orbit wobble Δt = −(2√μ / c²) × e × √a × sin E (IS-GPS-200, 20.3.3.3.3.1)

μ = 3.986005 × 10¹⁴ m³/s² is Earth’s gravity constant, R = 6,371 km, r the orbit radius from Earth’s centre, a the orbit’s semi-major axis, e its eccentricity and E the eccentric anomaly, found by solving Kepler’s equation M = E − e sin E with Newton’s method. The two effects cancel at r = 1.5 R, about 3,190 km up. At GPS radius (26,560 km) they come to −7.21 and +45.72 µs per day: +38.50 µs together. (A more complete model gives 38.575.) The real system builds this in: the satellite clocks are set to tick slightly slow before launch, and the control segment broadcasts corrections.

In the drift experiment, each satellite’s clock error is added to its pseudorange (a clock that runs ahead stamps its signals late, so the signal seems to have travelled less far) and the normal receiver solves for position and its own clock with eight satellites.

What we simplified

A spherical, non-rotating Earth and circular orbits in the first part. The “no correction for days” experiment is a thought experiment: real satellites are corrected all the time. The wobble uses an assumed semi-major axis of 26,560 km. Simulated curves, not historical measurements (the NTS-2 test satellite measured the real effect in 1977).
