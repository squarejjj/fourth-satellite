Spread beats bunched

Two satellites in the same part of the sky tell the receiver nearly the same thing. Spread out, each adds new information, and the circles cross at sharp angles.

Height is worst, because of the clock

Every satellite you can hear is above you. A slightly longer distance to all of them could mean you are lower, or that your clock is slow. The receiver can’t fully tell which, so height and clock errors grow together.

The formula agrees with the solver

The ellipse is drawn from a formula (DOP). The dots come from actually solving 300 noisy measurement sets. About 39% of dots fall inside the dashed ellipse and 95% inside the solid one.

How it works

The receiver solves the linearised equations y = G·x (see the solver lab). Each row of G is the direction to one satellite plus a 1 for the clock. If each measurement has independent error of size σ, the errors in east, north, up and clock have covariance σ²·Q, where

Q = (GᵀG)⁻¹ row of G = [ −cos(el)·sin(az), −cos(el)·cos(az), −sin(el), 1 ] HDOP = √(Q_ee + Q_nn) VDOP = √Q_uu PDOP = √(Q_ee + Q_nn + Q_uu) TDOP = √Q_tt position error (1σ) ≈ DOP × range error

Q depends only on the directions to the satellites, so a receiver can compute it before it measures anything. The dashed ellipse is σ·√(eigenvalues of the east–north block of Q); the solid one is 2.45 times bigger, which holds 95% of points for a two-dimensional normal spread.

The column of ones is the clock. When every satellite is above you, the “up” column of G is always negative too, so it looks a lot like the clock column, and the solver struggles to separate them. Ticking I know the exact time deletes that column. Ticking Galileo adds a second clock column for satellites 5–8, so one more satellite is needed.

What we simplified

The Earth is a sphere, the satellites are placed by direction on a 26,560 km sphere, and every measurement error is independent with the same size. Real errors are partly shared between satellites (the atmosphere) and partly not, and receivers weight satellites differently. The receiver is at 37.5°N 127°E (Seoul), as in our research notes.
