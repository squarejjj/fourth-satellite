Guess, check, correct

The receiver can’t solve its four equations directly: each contains a square root. So it guesses (the centre of the Earth will do), straightens each equation out at the guess, solves those easy straight-line equations, and repeats. Watch how fast the error collapses, and what happens with satellites much closer to the Earth.

A shortcut with a ghost

Bancroft’s method (1985) skips the guessing: squaring the equations turns everything into one quadratic. A quadratic has two answers. Usually one fails the original equations. Drag the three beacons into a straight line and both answers fit perfectly: the receiver can’t tell you from your mirror image.

How it works

For a guess (x, y, z, b), each satellite’s predicted pseudorange is |satellite − guess| + b. The receiver replaces that curved function by its tangent at the guess. Row i of the matrix G is the unit vector from satellite i to the guess, then a 1 for the clock, and it solves

G · Δ = measured − predicted Δ = (GᵀG)⁻¹ Gᵀ (measured − predicted) guess ← guess + Δ

If the guess is wrong by e, the tangent is wrong by only about e² ÷ (2 × distance to the satellite). With satellites 20,000 km away that factor is tiny, so each step squares the error and divides it by about 40,000 km. At 550 km the factor is about 30 times bigger, so a guess from the centre of the Earth is far less reliable: the NAVIGATION paper in our research found plain Gauss–Newton struggles there and adds a step-size factor below 1, as our safety net does.

Bancroft writes each equation with a “Lorentz” dot product ⟨a, b⟩ = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃ − a₄b₄ that subtracts the clock term, which turns the problem into ⟨u,u⟩Λ² + 2(⟨u,v⟩ − 1)Λ + ⟨v,v⟩ = 0. Each root gives a candidate position and clock; the receiver keeps the one that satisfies the original, unsquared equations. Our self-test checks that this agrees with Gauss–Newton.

What we simplified

Noise-free measurements, a spherical Earth, satellites placed by direction. For low orbits we keep the same sky directions and only change the height, which is not how a real low-orbit constellation looks.
