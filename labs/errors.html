How it works

The errors are independent: the ionosphere doesn’t know what the satellite clock is doing. Independent errors combine like steps in random directions, as the square root of the sum of their squares, so the biggest one dominates. A 7 m error and a 3.6 m error make 7.9 m, not 10.6 m. Removing the troposphere entirely (0.7 m) changes the 1995 total from 8.13 m to 8.10 m; removing the ionosphere brings it to 4.1 m.

UERE = √( σ_iono² + σ_tropo² + σ_clock+orbit² + σ_noise² + σ_multipath² ) sideways error (1σ) ≈ HDOP × UERE 2 drms ≈ 2 × HDOP × UERE (about 95%)

The receiver’s own clock isn’t in that list, because the solver removes it as the fourth unknown. Without that, a clock just 1 ms out puts about 300 km on every distance. It is by far the biggest error in GPS.

What we simplified

These are textbook typical values from a 1995 report, when the US still degraded civilian GPS on purpose (“selective availability”, switched off in May 2000). The 1995 budget predicts about 32 m sideways with SA off, yet GPS.gov says phones are typically within about 4.9 m in open sky. Satellites, clocks and orbits have improved a lot since, phones see more satellites, and they smooth their fixes; we could not reconcile the two from our sources, so treat these as textbook numbers, not what your phone gets. The “city street” value uses the ~20 m one-sigma L1 errors measured on a phone in Hong Kong street canyons, where echoes break the “independent, zero-average” assumption anyway (see Lab 5).
