How it works

GPS signals cross 20,000 km and arrive far weaker than the background noise (see Lab 1). A jammer just adds noise: each satellite is lost once its signal-to-noise ratio drops below a threshold, low satellites first, and with fewer than four the phone has no fresh position.

A spoofer computes, for a fake position, the pseudorange every satellite would give, and broadcasts signals with those timings. Done carefully, the fake measurements agree with each other perfectly, so the residual check (Lab 5) sees nothing wrong: the receiver itself can’t tell. A careless spoofer that fakes only some satellites leaves them disagreeing with the real ones, and the residual check fires.

The second check uses something the attacker doesn’t control: the car’s own wheel speed and compass, added up over time (“dead reckoning”). They drift slowly, so the phone allows some disagreement; if GPS and the wheels part by more than that, the position is flagged as suspicious. Neither check is a guarantee: a slow enough spoofer can hide inside the allowed disagreement.

What we simplified

One fixed sky of 8 satellites, 2 m of normal noise, a straight road, and a spoofer that takes over instantly and pulls the position north at a steady rate. The wheel estimate has a 1% speed error and a 1° compass error. Real receivers also watch signal power, clock jumps and other sensors, and signal authentication (such as Galileo’s OSNMA) proves a message is genuine but not that its timing wasn’t delayed.
