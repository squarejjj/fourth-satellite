Lab 4 · The ionosphere

A delay your phone partly doesn’t mind

Between 50 and 1,000 km up, sunlight strips electrons off the thin air. That charged layer slows the GPS code by a few metres’ worth, more in the afternoon and more for satellites low in the sky. Part of that delay is the same for every satellite, and the clock unknown soaks it up.

Add the ionosphere to eight satellites and let the receiver solve. If every satellite were delayed by the same amount, the solver would put all of it into the clock, the fourth unknown, and your position wouldn’t move. The real delay is bigger for low satellites, and that difference is what moves you, mostly in height.

3. One frequency or two

The delay depends on frequency: 40.3 × (electrons per m²) ÷ frequency². So a phone that receives both L1 (1575.42 MHz) and L5 (1176.45 MHz) sees the same signal arrive at two different times, and the gap between them gives the delay away. The catch: the noise of both measurements gets mixed in, multiplied by 2.59.

How it works

The Klobuchar model is a cosine-shaped hump of delay peaking at 14:00 local time on top of a constant 5 ns at night, stretched by a slant factor because a low signal crosses more of the layer:

delay = F × [ 5 ns + A × (1 − x²/2 + x⁴/24) ] by day (|x| < 1.57) x = 2π(t − 50,400 s)/P delay = F × 5 ns at night F = 1 + 16 × (0.53 − elevation)³ (elevation in semicircles: 90° = 0.5)

A and P come from the eight numbers in the broadcast message and the place where the signal crosses the layer. With two frequencies the receiver needs no model at all:

R1 = ρ + I R5 = ρ + γ·I γ = (f1/f5)² = 1.7933 I = (R5 − R1) / (γ − 1) ρ = 2.2606·R1 − 1.2606·R5 (noise × √(2.2606² + 1.2606²) = × 2.588)
What we simplified

We use sample Klobuchar coefficients that our research notes took from a documentation example, not live ones, and the model only removes about half the real delay worldwide (Navipedia’s figure). In part 2 the delay grows with elevation through the slant factor alone, the same for every direction; the real ionosphere also varies across the sky. In part 3 the noise is independent on each frequency, and only the first-order delay is modelled.
