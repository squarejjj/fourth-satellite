Lost in the city

Between tall buildings, many satellites are hidden, and some of the ones you “hear” are only echoes off a glass wall. An echo has travelled farther, so it always arrives late. The phone can’t tell an echo from the real thing, so it thinks that satellite is farther away than it is. Raise the buildings and watch the fix jump.

The check needs spare satellites: with more satellites than unknowns, the measurements must agree, so a bad one shows up as a large mismatch.

Try this

Raise the buildings to about 70 m and watch the orange fix jump across the street. Then switch on the residual check, and drag yourself across the street: sometimes the check finds the echo, sometimes the echoes agree with each other and it can’t.

When the direct signal still gets through

If the direct signal arrives and an echo arrives a moment later, the receiver sees a blend. The echo bends the peak that the timing loop locks onto (see Lab 1), so the measurement shifts a little, early or late depending on the echo’s phase.

How it works

The buildings run along the street forever, so whether a satellite is hidden depends only on how steeply its signal crosses the street. For a satellite at azimuth az and elevation el, the signal climbs tan(el) / |sin az| metres for every metre it moves across the street. If it hasn’t cleared the roof by the time it reaches the wall, it is blocked.

A blocked signal can still arrive by bouncing off the building on the other side. The bounce is worked out with the mirror trick: reflect the phone in the wall, and the bounced path is as long as the straight line from the mirror image. For a far-away satellite the extra distance is

extra path = 2 × (distance to the reflecting wall) × cos(el) × |sin az|

That extra distance is added to the satellite’s pseudorange and handed to the same receiver as everywhere else. Because it is always positive and can be tens of metres, it breaks the assumption behind the error budget (small, independent, averaging to zero). The residual check solves with all satellites, and if the mismatches are much bigger than normal noise (here: over 3 m), drops the worst one and tries again.

For the lower panel, the receiver’s timing loop compares the correlation half a spacing early and half a spacing late and settles where they balance. An echo adds a second, delayed triangle (scaled by its strength, flipped if it arrives out of step). Echoes longer than 1.5 chips don’t touch the peak at all: 440 m for L1 but only 44 m for L5, whose chips are ten times shorter.

What we simplified

Buildings are two infinitely long smooth walls that reflect perfectly, each satellite gives at most one echo, and the phone has 1 m of ordinary noise per satellite. Real receivers also lose weak signals, and real echoes come off many surfaces. The residual check is the simplest form of the idea (RAIM-style fault exclusion); it is not what any particular phone runs. The correlation model is idealised: one echo, a perfect code and infinite bandwidth.
